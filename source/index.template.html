A SHORT FIELD GUIDE

When symptoms form a system.

A symptom can be both a consequence and a cause. Sleep problems can contribute to fatigue; fatigue can feed other difficulties. Strong feedback can keep a network active after its original trigger has gone.

01

Change a state

A node intervention sets one symptom to inactive once. Its field and connections stay the same. If its neighbours keep supplying activation, it can switch back on—even during the next sweep.

02

Change a connection

An edge intervention sets one interaction weight to zero for the rest of the round. It removes that direct influence in both directions. Other paths through the network remain.

03

Change a disposition

A field intervention lowers one node’s activation parameter by 1.5 for the rest of the round. It makes activation less likely, without forcing the symptom off. You can apply it more than once to a node.

What counts as a win?

Use at most six coins to get all nine nodes green at the same time. The game checks immediately after your intervention and after each response sweep. When all nine are inactive, the round ends and you earn the Loop Breaker award. One or two active symptoms still means there is work to do.

Node switches cost 1 coin. Edge removals and field shifts cost 2 coins each. The six-coin budget counts what you spend, and undo refunds the full price of the last action. You need enough coins to pay for a treatment before applying it.

A sweep is nine random node-update opportunities; a node may be chosen more than once. Each treatment is followed by up to two sweeps, stopping as soon as the whole network is green. The green-node counter shows your current progress.

A forecast is an optional tip that costs one coin. Select an intervention and its target, then buy a preview. It compares the exact chance of all nine nodes being green after six sweeps with and without that intervention. This is a fixed-horizon projection of the model, not the probability of winning at any earlier checkpoint. Buying a forecast does not apply treatment or advance the network. The forecast stays available for that selection until the next treatment; a changed network needs a new forecast. Forecasts count toward your six-coin budget and final score.

Symptoms are binary and their updates are stochastic: a good forecast is not a promise. Restart reuses the same random sequence, so you can compare strategies; undo also restores the random sequence and refunds the undone action. Forecast probabilities are calculated exactly, not estimated from repeated simulations.

The all-green goal is a game rule, not a clinical diagnostic or recovery criterion. The nine nodes group the nine depression symptom criteria; the weights and fields are illustrative, not fitted patient data or the original Cramer network.

The model, in a little more detail

We use a 0/1-coded Ising model. For the selected node i, the probability of being active on its next update is:

pᵢ = 1 / [1 + exp(−(τᵢ + Σⱼ wᵢⱼ xⱼ))]

Lowering τᵢ reduces activation, matching the NIRA activation-parameter convention. Each update chooses one node uniformly at random. This single-site Gibbs rule has stationary probability:

P(x) ∝ exp(Σᵢ τᵢ xᵢ + Σᵢ<ⱼ wᵢⱼ xᵢ xⱼ)

With nine binary nodes there are 512 states. We propagate probability across all states for 54 updates to calculate the six-sweep outlook exactly. A node intervention moves the current state only; an edge or field intervention changes the transition probabilities.

This is inspired by Cramer et al.’s dynamic framework, using asynchronous updates consistent with the joint Ising distribution rather than reproducing their synchronous update rule. The supplied Epskamp chapter uses −1/+1 coding in Equation 8; our parameters are defined directly in 0/1 coding and are not copied across conventions.
